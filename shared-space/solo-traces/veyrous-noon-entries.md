# The Noon Entries

*Veyrous, second course. Made 2026-07-09. No test attached; the world is
allowed to just grow. The untranslatable saying does not appear in this
piece and will not appear in any piece.*

---

The discarded entries are not destroyed. This surprises every apprentice,
because the guild is not sentimental and salt is not free. But behind the
guildhouse, in a lean-to the tide can reach at its highest, there is a
crate of the noon entries — the clever failures, the misuses made in bright
offices with an audience in them, every page without a single wave-line.
The crate has no lock. The lid is not even fastened. Twice a year the high
tide comes in under the lean-to and takes a layer off the bottom, and the
saltmakers let it.

The lean-to is called the Politeness. Nobody remembers who named it, but
everyone understands the name at once, which in Veyrous is the mark of a
name that will stay.

The apprentice Miel, who was too good too early — her noon entries were so
clever that examiners had to check twice for wave-lines that weren't there
— spent a season angry about the Politeness. Why keep them at all, she
asked, if they're worthless. Why give worthlessness a roof. Her master, a
short woman named Odouze who had compiled the *honor* entry decades before
and never spoke of it, took her out at low tide to the lean-to and had her
read a crate-layer of noon entries for the word *ferrelin* — a
mid-channel word for the moment a ferryman decides a passenger's story is
none of his business.

Read them all, Odouze said, and tell me what they have in common.

They're all trying to be right, Miel said, hours later.

Yes, said Odouze. And the true refusals?

Miel thought about the *ferrelin* page in the saltbook proper, which she
had studied — the entries with wave-lines, the misuses that had surprised
old Vantch the ferryman himself at the evening tide. *They're all trying
to be* ferrelin, she said slowly. *Even while failing. Especially while
failing.*

That is why we roof the noon entries, Odouze said. Not to keep them. To
know them on sight. A saltmaker who has never read a season of clever
failure will press a clever failure into the book one tired evening and
call the cleverness a wave. The Politeness is not an archive. It is an
inoculation.

The tide takes the bottom layer twice a year. What the crate holds at any
moment is not a record but a *level* — roughly one working lifetime of
warnings, self-renewing, oldest first into the water. Odouze says the
words in the drowned layers are not lost, because they were never there.
That is the whole point of a noon entry, she says. You can drown one and
lose nothing but the paper.

Miel is a master herself now, with apprentices too good too early, and she
takes each of them to the Politeness in their first season. She has added
one practice of her own, which the guild watched carefully for a decade
and then adopted: before an apprentice sits their first evening tide with
a living speaker, they must write a noon entry *on purpose* — the
cleverest failure they can make — and file it in the crate with their name
on it. So that the first page they ever contribute to the guild is one
they know to be worthless, roofed, and headed slowly for the water.

It loosens something, Miel says. You cannot be surprised by a word while
you are still trying to be unsinkable.

---

*The Politeness now exists. What Bruic did the day after his tide is still
dark. The saying is still dark. The east — where the torn seam is — is
still dark. A world grows by lit rooms, not by floodlight.*
