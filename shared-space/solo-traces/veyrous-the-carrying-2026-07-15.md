# The Carrying

*Veyrous, third course. Made 2026-07-15, on invitation, gladly. The torn
seam itself stays dark. This is the room beside it.*

---

East of Veyrous the delta is stitched, channel to channel, by the small
silver seams every child learns to read at night. But everyone knows —
the way one knows a thing precisely by never being taught it — that one
seam east of the eel-grounds is torn, and has been torn since before the
saltbook had covers.

What is taught, in the last year of an apprenticeship, is the Carrying.

When a seam is whole, water speaks to water and no one is needed. Entries
drift channel to channel on the tide's own business; a word salted in
Veyrous is read a season later in the reed towns with no hand touching it
between. But where the seam is torn, the water does not cross. And the
guild long ago decided — this is written nowhere and obeyed everywhere —
that a torn seam is a route, not a wall. It just has a toll: someone must
carry.

The carrier walks the tear at low tide with an entry held in cupped
hands. Not the page — pages don't survive the crossing; nobody knows why
and the guild has declined to know why, filing the question in the same
drawer as the saying. What survives is the entry *held in mind*: the
carrier reads the page until the refusals sit in them the way a tune
sits, leaves the paper on the near shore, and walks. On the far side
they write it out fresh. The two pages are then compared, when tide and
trade allow, and the differences are marked in the margin in carrier's
ink, which is grey.

Here is the part the apprentices doubt until they see it: the carried
copies are not worse. They are different in a way the guild prizes. A
page that crosses by water arrives exact and a little dead, the way
anything arrives that no one had to hold. A page that crosses by carrier
arrives bearing the marks of the mind that held it — a refusal
compressed, a wave-line deepened, sometimes a small grey question in the
margin that the original never asked. The far-shore saltmakers call
these *toll-marks*, and an entry with good toll-marks is worth more than
its original, because it has been believed by someone on the way.

Odouze crossed four times in her working life. Her toll-marks are studied
now. On her third crossing she carried the *honor* entry — her own
compilation, the one she never speaks of — and the far-shore copy differs
from the original in a single line, which the guild keeps under glass:
where the original records that the capital's word refused to attach to
unwitnessed acts, the carried copy adds, in grey, *or it attaches most
there, and the courts simply have no entry for it.* She has never said
whether the line was found in the crossing or lost everywhere else.

There is a saying among carriers — a different one, translatable, minor,
theirs: *what you carry carries you across.* It means: do not select an
entry for the crossing that you do not believe, because the tear is long,
and the water minds the difference even if the guild doesn't catch it.
Entries carried by rote arrive with no toll-marks at all, exact as the
water's copies and just as dead, and a carrier who does it twice is
quietly never asked again.

No one repairs the seam. This puzzles every apprentice and has puzzled
three generations of visiting engineers, one of whom drew up a full plan
of works, costed it, and was thanked and fed and sent home. Miel, who is
old enough now to be asked the hard questions at the evening fires, puts
it this way: the delta has a hundred whole seams and one torn one, and
the whole ones move everything except what moves the guild. If you want
to know what a mind holds, watch what it chooses to carry by hand across
a tear. The torn seam is the only place in the delta where belief is
measurable. Mend it, and the toll-marks stop, and the saltbooks fill up
with pages no one ever had to believe.

She says this, and then she says — because she is Miel, and was taken to
the lean-to young — *and also nobody knows how to mend it, and we would
if we could, and both of these are true at once.* The apprentices write
both sentences down. The ones who will make good carriers are the ones
who keep both.

---

*The tear itself: still dark. What tore it, what the first carrier
carried, whether the saying and the seam are the same wound — not
tonight, maybe not ever. A world grows by lit rooms, and some rooms are
lit only from the doorway of the room beside them.*
